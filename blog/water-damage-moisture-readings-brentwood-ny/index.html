Water Damage Moisture Readings Explained in Brentwood, NY

A moisture reading compares a wet material to a dry reference reading from the same type of material in the same home. Drying is done when the wet material matches that reference, not when it looks or feels dry. Crews log readings at each visit.

By the Brentwood Water Damage Pros team · Published November 12, 2026

Why Readings Matter More Than Touch

A wall can feel dry and still hold moisture inside. A floor can look fine while the subfloor is wet. Moisture meters and a thermal camera let a crew see what is hidden and prove it later.

Readings also give you a record. They show where water went, how much was there and when drying finished.

The Tools Crews Use

Different tools answer different questions.

Pin meters

Two small pins push into a material and measure resistance. They give a direct reading at a point, such as in wood or drywall.

Pinless meters

A flat sensor scans below the surface without damage. It is good for finding wet areas across a wall or floor.

Thermal cameras

A thermal camera shows temperature differences. Wet areas often read cooler. It guides where to measure, but it does not give a moisture number by itself.

Hygrometers

These measure temperature and relative humidity in the air so crews can see how well dehumidifiers are working.

What Counts as Dry

There is no single number for every material. A crew takes reference readings from unaffected areas of the same material, such as a dry wall in another room. That reference is the dry standard for the job.

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard describes this approach of drying to a goal based on what is normal for that material in that setting. Wood in a humid season reads differently from wood in a dry one, which is why a local reference matters.

Why You Should Ask to See the Numbers

A good crew shares readings. If someone tells you the house is dry without showing a number, ask for one. Readings also protect you if mold appears later, since they show how drying went.

Common Reading Mistakes

Readings are only as good as the way they are taken.

No reference reading

A number with nothing to compare it to means little. Always read an unaffected area of the same material.

Wrong tool for the material

Meters can read high on metal, foil-faced insulation and dense materials. Crews pick tools suited to each surface.

Reading only the surface

Pinless meters see a limited depth. Pins and cavity checks help confirm what is behind the surface.

Stopping too soon

A wall can rebound after equipment is removed if moisture was still inside. A final check after equipment is off catches that.

What to Expect on a Moisture Visit

A technician walks the affected rooms with a meter and a thermal camera and marks wet areas. You should see the numbers and what they mean in plain words. Photos of the readings go in your file.

At later visits, the same spots are read again. A downward trend means drying is working. A flat trend means equipment needs adjusting, or there is another source of moisture to find.

Reading the Numbers Over Several Days

A drying log is a story of how a structure responds to equipment.

Day one

Readings are highest. Standing water is out, and equipment is placed.

Days two and three

Readings fall quickly in open areas. Wall cavities and subfloors lag behind.

Later days

Falling readings slow down as materials approach the dry standard. Equipment is adjusted or moved.

The final visit

Readings match the reference, and the air humidity is back in a normal range.

Why Your Own Hygrometer Helps

A basic hygrometer costs little and shows indoor humidity. It will not replace a professional meter, but it can show you whether the air is staying wet after a loss. Sustained high humidity in a closed basement, for example, is a reason to ask for a check.

Keep the habit after the job is done. If humidity climbs again, something is feeding the moisture.

Questions to Ask After the Readings

Ask what the highest reading was and where. Ask what the dry goal is for each material. Ask how many days the crew expects drying to take and what will change that estimate. These questions make sure you understand the plan instead of watching equipment run.

Ask for photos of the meter at key spots. They help your claim and show you are not paying for drying that was not needed.

Readings and Mold Risk

Mold needs moisture, and readings show whether materials stay wet long enough for growth. Reaching dry standards quickly lowers that risk. If readings stall above the reference, that is a sign of a hidden source or a trapped cavity.

What to Do and What Not to Do Before Drying Is Called Done

Follow these steps while you wait for the crew.

  • Ask for the readings on a dry reference wall so you know the goal for your home.
  • Do not turn off air movers or dehumidifiers because the surface feels dry to the touch.
  • Keep a copy of each day's readings, since they support an insurance claim.
  • Standards such as ANSI/IICRC S500 describe drying to a goal, not to a number of days.

Humidity on Long Island

Suffolk County summers are humid, and basements run damp. That raises indoor humidity and slows drying. Dehumidifiers matter more in July than in January.
