How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Brentwood, NY

A scope of work is the written list of everything a restoration company will do. Read it by phase: extraction, demolition, drying, cleaning and rebuild. Each line should name the room, the material and the unit of measure so you can check it against the damage.

By the Brentwood Water Damage Pros team · Published November 12, 2026

What a Scope of Work Is

A scope of work lists the tasks, the locations and the quantities. It is different from an estimate, which attaches prices to those tasks. The scope is what your insurer reviews to decide what they will pay for.

Reading it by phase makes it easier to follow.

Reading a Scope in Four Phases

Extraction

Water removal by room and method.

Demolition

Materials removed such as pad, drywall and insulation.

Drying

Equipment type, count and days with monitoring visits.

Rebuild

New materials and finishes.

Common Scope Line Items

Line itemWhat it meansUnit
Water extraction.Removing standing or absorbed water.Hours or square feet.
Carpet pad removal.Disposing of wet pad.Square feet.
Drywall removal.Cutting out wet drywall.Square feet or linear feet.
Air mover.Equipment to move air across wet surfaces.Per unit per day.
Dehumidifier.Equipment to remove moisture from air.Per unit per day.
Monitoring.Visits to take readings.Per visit.
Drywall replace and paint.Rebuild after drying.Square feet.

What to Question

Ask about anything that does not match what you see.

Quantities

Does the square footage match the room? A wall of 100 square feet should not be listed as 400.

Days of equipment

Does the number of drying days match the readings? Ask for the log.

Items you did not see

If a material is listed that was not wet, ask why it is removed.

Missing items

If a wet area is not in the scope, ask whether it was read and found dry.

How the Scope Changes During the Job

A scope is a starting point. Once walls are opened, hidden damage can appear, and a good company documents each change with photos and readings. Ask how change approvals work.

Drying also changes the scope. If readings are dry sooner, equipment days go down. If they are slow, days go up.

Scope and Your Insurer

Insurers often compare a company's scope with their own. Differences are normal, and a clear scope with readings and photos makes them easier to resolve. See ceiling water damage repair and wall and drywall water damage repair for two common scope areas.

A Sample Walk Through a Basement Scope

Picture a finished basement with an inch of water from a failed water heater.

Extraction

The scope lists extraction of the basement floor area, usually by hours or square feet.

Demolition

Wet carpet and pad are removed. Drywall is cut out a set height above the floor, often a couple of feet, and wet insulation comes out.

Drying

Air movers and dehumidifiers are listed by count with the number of days and monitoring visits.

Rebuild

Drywall, baseboard, paint and flooring are listed separately with square footage.

Ask for the Photos and Readings That Support the Scope

Each demolition line should have a reason you can see. A photo of wet drywall with a moisture reading beside it is stronger than a line item alone.

If a company cannot show why a wall is coming out, ask for a reading. If the reading is dry, the wall may be saved. If it is wet, you have the justification for your insurer.

A Quick Scope Review Checklist

  • Every wet room is listed.
  • Quantities match what you can measure.
  • Equipment days match the readings.
  • Rebuild lines are separate from mitigation lines.
  • Exclusions are stated.

Scope Terms You May See

A few words come up often.

Mitigation

Work done to stop further damage, such as extraction, removal of wet materials and drying.

Reconstruction

Work that rebuilds what was removed.

Contents

Belongings in the home, handled separately from the structure.

Category and class

Category describes the water's contamination. Class describes how much material is wet. Both come from the ANSI/IICRC S500 approach.

Scope for a Business or Rental

Commercial scopes often add ceiling tiles, carpet tile and stock. Rental scopes may separate the landlord's building items from the tenant's belongings. Ask which party each line belongs to so claims go to the right insurer.

Scope Gaps Between Mitigation and Rebuild

Gaps show up when mitigation removes materials and nobody lists the rebuild. A wall cut out two feet high needs new drywall, tape, texture, primer and paint. A removed floor needs a new floor and trim.

Ask for a rebuild scope or a clear note on who will handle it. Without it, you may be left with open walls and no plan.

Keeping the Scope Current

Ask for an updated scope whenever the plan changes, and keep each version with its date. When the job ends, compare the final scope with the first. The differences should match the photos and notes you collected along the way.

A clean paper trail makes insurance conversations simpler and gives you a record for the home's future.

Reading the Notes at the Bottom

Scopes often end with notes and assumptions. Read them closely. They may say that pricing assumes standard access, that hidden damage will be priced separately or that contents are not included. Each note tells you where the number could change.

Ask the company to explain any note that is unclear before you sign, and ask for the answer in writing.

Questions to Bring to the Walk-Through

Walk the property with the estimator. Ask them to point to each wet area and show the reading. Ask which items they plan to remove and why, and which they plan to dry in place. Take notes and photos as you go.

A clear walk-through makes the written scope easy to check later because you already know what each line refers to.

Local Notes for Brentwood

Brentwood homes often have finished basements and older wall construction. Scope items there often include insulation and trim. If structural work is involved, ask the Town of Islip Building Division about permits.
