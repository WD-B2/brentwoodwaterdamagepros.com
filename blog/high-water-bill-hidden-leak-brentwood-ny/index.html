Sudden Jump in a Brentwood, NY Water Bill: Hidden Leaks

A sudden rise in your water bill with no change in use can mean a hidden leak. Turn off every fixture and see if the meter still moves. Check toilets, then look for warm floor spots, damp walls and the sound of running water.

By the Brentwood Water Damage Pros team · Published November 12, 2026

Why a Bill Can Rise Without a Visible Leak

Many leaks never show on the surface. A pipe in a wall, a line under a slab or a toilet that runs a thin stream can use water for weeks before anyone notices. The bill is often the first clue.

Compare the last few bills for the same season. A jump that does not match higher use is a reason to look.

A Four-Step Leak Check

Stop all use

Turn off every fixture, appliance and irrigation system.

Read the meter

Check whether the meter or the leak indicator still moves.

Test the toilets

Add food coloring to the tank and see if it reaches the bowl without flushing.

Inspect

Check under sinks, around the water heater and along walls and floors.

What Your Water Utility Can and Cannot Tell You

Your utility can confirm that usage rose. It cannot find the pipe. The Suffolk County Water Authority says its meters are read remotely by radio, so customers do not need to read them, though you can still check your own meter for movement. If your address uses another provider, ask them how to check.

Ask your utility whether an adjustment is available for leak-related usage. Policies vary.

Slab and Underground Leaks

A line under the floor is hard to find and easy to miss.

Signs

Warm patches on tile, hissing or running sounds with fixtures off and a persistent damp smell.

What follows

A plumber finds and repairs the leak. A restoration crew dries the floor and walls. See slab leak water damage.

Leaks Inside Walls

A pinhole leak in a supply line inside a wall can wet insulation and drywall slowly. Signs include a stain that grows, paint that blisters and a smell that does not go away. See wall and drywall water damage repair for what happens after the plumber stops the leak.

Reading the Meter Step by Step

Your meter holds the evidence.

Find the meter

It is usually near where the water line enters the house, in a basement, utility room or a box in the yard.

Look for a leak indicator

Many meters have a small dial or triangle that spins when any water moves. If it spins with everything off, water is leaking.

Record and wait

Note the reading, wait an hour with nothing running and read again. A change means a leak.

Narrow it down

Close the main shutoff to the house and check whether the meter still moves. If it does, the leak is between the meter and the house.

Outdoor and Irrigation Leaks

A broken sprinkler line or a leaking outdoor spigot can push water use up without any sign indoors. Walk the yard after the system runs and look for soggy patches or unusually green strips. Water from an irrigation break can also flow toward a foundation or into a crawl space, which is one way water ends up under a house.

See crawl space water damage if water has reached the space under the house.

What to Do With an Unusually High Bill

Take these steps in order.

Check usage history

Compare to the same months last year to account for seasons.

Test for leaks

Use the meter and dye test.

Call a plumber

If you find a leak or cannot find one, a plumber can locate it.

Dry damage promptly

Wet materials left in place cause a second cost beyond the water bill.

A Leak Found Is a Leak to Dry

Finding a leak is half the job. If the water wet a wall, floor or ceiling, the structure still needs drying. Drywall, insulation and subfloor soaked for weeks will hold moisture after the plumber leaves.

A short moisture check after the repair tells you whether more work is needed. Ask for readings that show the wall or floor is back to normal.

What a Leak Can Cost Beyond the Bill

A hidden leak costs twice. The first cost is the water you paid for. The second is the damage it caused to drywall, flooring and framing. A slow leak can run for months before a stain appears.

Fixing the leak promptly limits both costs. Once the plumber is done, have the surrounding materials checked with a moisture meter so you know whether drying or repair is needed.

Keep Bills and Readings

Keep your recent bills and your meter notes. A plumber and your insurer may both ask for them, and they show when the leak likely started.

How Drying Standards Apply Once the Leak Is Found

Finding the leak is plumbing work. Drying what it soaked follows the ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage guide, which sets drying goals by material. A slow leak that stayed hidden for weeks is usually treated as longer exposure, and that affects whether drywall and insulation can be saved.

Local Notes for Brentwood

The Suffolk County Water Authority says its water comes from underground aquifers and that even a small drip can waste dozens of gallons daily. That makes small leaks worth chasing.

Brentwood homes may have older plumbing, so an annual look under sinks and around the water heater is a good habit. Our crews also work in East Islip and Islip Terrace.
