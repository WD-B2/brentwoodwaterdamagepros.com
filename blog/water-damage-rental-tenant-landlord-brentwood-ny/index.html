Water Damage in a Brentwood, NY Rental: Who Does What

Tenants should stop the water if safe, photograph the damage and notify the landlord in writing. New York law implies a warranty of habitability in residential leases, and landlords generally must repair leaks that affect habitability. Renter's insurance usually covers belongings, not the building.

By the Brentwood Water Damage Pros team · Published November 12, 2026

Why Rentals Are Different

In a rental, the building belongs to the landlord and the belongings belong to the tenant. That split decides who files what. It also makes written records more important than in an owner-occupied home.

This guide is general information and not legal advice. Housing legal aid or an attorney can advise on your situation.

What the Law Says in Brief

New York Real Property Law section 235-b implies a warranty of habitability in every residential lease.

The warranty

Legal Aid sources describe it as requiring a unit fit for human habitation and free from conditions dangerous or detrimental to life, health or safety. Water leaks are among the conditions it covers.

Written notice

Tenant guidance recommends asking for repairs in writing and keeping a copy, plus photos and videos.

Be careful with withholding rent

Withholding rent or repair-and-deduct can be risky. Ask a tenant attorney or housing agency before doing either.

Make it safe

Shut off the source and keep clear of wet electrical.

Document

Photograph damage and belongings.

Notify in writing

Send a dated message or letter to the landlord and keep a copy.

What a Landlord Should Do

A fast response limits damage and disputes.

Fix the source

Call a plumber or roofer quickly.

Start mitigation

Document and notify

Keep records and notify your insurer. Share information with the tenant.

A Sample Timeline for a Rental Leak

Speed and paperwork matter.

Hour one

Stop the water if safe, take photos and message the landlord.

Day one

Send a written notice, call renter's insurance and move belongings to a dry place.

Days two to five

Landlord arranges plumbing repair and drying. Tenant keeps records.

Weeks later

Repairs finish and the tenant confirms the area is dry and safe.

Renter's Insurance Basics

Renter's insurance typically covers your belongings and sometimes extra living costs. It generally does not pay for the building. Ask your insurer how a water loss is handled and whether you need to report right away.

If a neighbor's unit caused the leak, ask about liability coverage on their side. Keep photos of every damaged item.

Basement and Shared Space Situations

Rentals with shared walls or basements raise special questions.

Shared plumbing

A leak from another unit may affect yours. Tell the landlord which wall or ceiling is wet.

Basement units

Water tends to collect there. Ask about sump pumps and drainage.

Mold in a rental

Do not scrub large areas. Notify the landlord in writing and ask a housing advocate about your options.

Landlord Records That Help Everyone

A landlord who keeps photos, moisture logs and invoices protects themselves in disputes and insurance claims. Share copies with the tenant when appropriate. A calm, documented process reduces conflict and gets the unit back in use faster.

Communication That Helps Both Sides

Most disputes grow from silence.

Tenants

Send dated messages with photos. Say what you need and by when.

Landlords

Reply quickly with a plan, even if the plan is to send a plumber the next day.

Both

Keep copies of every message and invoice.

Safety Issues That Cannot Wait

Some situations are urgent: water near electrical outlets, a ceiling that is bulging, sewage on the floor or no heat in winter after a pipe failure. If these happen, call the landlord at once and call emergency services if there is danger.

Do not stay in a unit with active electrical hazards. Take photos from a safe spot and report them.

When the Landlord Does Not Respond

Tenant guidance suggests making the request in writing, documenting conditions and allowing a reasonable time. Legal aid organizations describe options including suing the landlord for repairs. Rent withholding and repair-and-deduct are risky.

Talk to a tenant attorney or a local housing organization before acting. This page is not legal advice.

Moving Out After a Major Loss

A major loss may mean temporary relocation. Ask the landlord and your renter's insurer about extra living costs, and check your lease for rent abatement terms. Take photos of the unit when you leave and keep copies of every communication.

If the unit is not safe, a housing advocate or an attorney can explain your options. Do not sign a release or a lease change without reading it carefully.

Keep receipts for hotel stays, storage and meals, since some policies reimburse them.

Records Both Sides Should Keep

Keep dated photos of the leak and of every damaged area, the written notice to the landlord and any replies, invoices for repairs and drying, and the renter's insurance claim number if one was opened. Store copies somewhere other than the affected unit.

If a dispute arises later, this file is what a mediator, court or insurer will want to see.

Short Checklist for Landlords

Fix the source, start drying within a day, notify your insurer, share updates with the tenant and keep every invoice. Quick action protects the building and the relationship, and it keeps small leaks from becoming habitability disputes.

What to Do and What Not to Do as a Tenant After a Leak

Follow these steps while you wait for the crew.

  • Tell the landlord in writing right away and keep a copy with the date.
  • Photograph the damage and your belongings before moving anything.
  • Do not deduct rent or tear out wet drywall on your own without a written agreement.
  • Check whether your renter's policy covers your belongings, since the landlord's policy usually does not.

The Standard a Fair Repair Scope Should Follow

A written scope for a rental repair should reference the ANSI/IICRC S500 drying approach and list readings that show completion. Tenants and landlords can both point to that benchmark when a dispute arises. It is a reference for the work, not a legal ruling.
