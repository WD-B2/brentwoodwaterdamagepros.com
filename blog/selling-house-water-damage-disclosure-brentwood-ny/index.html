Selling a Brentwood, NY House After Water Damage: Disclosure

New York sellers answer a property condition disclosure statement that asks about water penetration and flooding. The law was amended effective March 20, 2024 to add flood questions and remove the $500 credit. Keep repair records, and ask a real estate attorney how it applies to you.

By the Brentwood Water Damage Pros team · Published November 12, 2026

Water Damage and a Home Sale

A past leak does not have to sink a sale, but hiding it can. Buyers, lenders and inspectors look for water stains, musty smells and mold. A house with documented repairs usually reads better than one with unexplained patches.

This guide is general information. It is not legal advice, so talk to a real estate attorney about your situation.

What Changed in the Disclosure Law

New York's Property Condition Disclosure Act was amended by Chapter 484 of the Laws of 2023.

The $500 credit was removed

According to the New York State Bar Association, the amendment removed the requirement that a seller give the buyer a $500 credit if a statement was not delivered before a binding contract.

Flood questions were added

The statement now asks whether the property is in a 100-year or 500-year floodplain based on FEMA maps, whether flood insurance is required or in place and whether a flood claim was filed.

Water penetration

A question was changed to ask about any water penetration or damage, rather than only standing water. The amended statement took effect March 20, 2024.

Mold, Lenders and Clearance

Some lenders will not fund a purchase until mold is removed. Independent clearance from a licensed assessor documents the result. We make no promises about what a lender will accept.

In New York, the assessor and the remediator must be different companies on the same project. See mold remediation for how that works.

What to Do Before You List

A short checklist reduces surprises.

Fix the source first

Have a plumber or roofer confirm the leak is fixed.

Dry and repair properly

Cosmetic patches over damp walls draw questions. See wall and drywall water damage repair.

Collect the paperwork

Put invoices, readings and permits in one folder.

Check the flood map

Look up your address on the FEMA flood map so you can answer the flood questions accurately.

If You Are Buying

A buyer should ask for repair records, look for staining and ask for a moisture reading during inspection. A water stain is not a verdict, but it is a question.

Talking to Buyers About Past Water Damage

Honest, documented answers reassure more than silence.

Share the story

Explain what happened, when and how it was fixed.

Show the paper

Hand over invoices, readings and permits.

Offer access

Let inspectors see the area. Hidden problems worry buyers more than known, fixed ones.

Ask your attorney

A real estate attorney can tell you how to answer the disclosure questions accurately.

What Buyers and Inspectors Look For

Inspectors look for stains on ceilings and walls, soft spots on floors, musty smells in basements and closets and signs of past repairs such as fresh paint in one spot. They may use a moisture meter.

A documented repair with readings answers those questions. An undocumented one invites more.

Questions Buyers Commonly Ask

  • What caused the water damage, and was the source fixed?
  • Was a professional drying crew used?
  • Was an insurance claim filed?
  • Were permits pulled for any rebuild?
  • Has any mold been found or removed?

Insurance History and Your Sale

The amended disclosure statement asks about flood claims, and buyers may ask for more.

Claims history reports

Insurers share claim histories through industry databases that buyers' insurers may check.

Flood insurance

If your property is in a mapped floodplain and has a mortgage, flood insurance may be required.

Be accurate

Accurate answers protect you more than guesses.

Preparing the Basement and Crawl Space for a Showing

Basements and crawl spaces are where buyers look. Clear stored items from walls so inspectors can see the surfaces. Make sure sump pumps work, floor drains are clear and dehumidifiers are running if needed.

A dry, odor-free basement with a few documents on a table tells a better story than a freshly painted one with no records. If you had past water in the space, see basement water damage cleanup for what proper restoration involves.

If an Inspection Finds Moisture

If a buyer's inspector finds moisture, do not panic. Ask for the readings, get your own assessment and fix the source. A dry reading after repair is useful negotiating evidence. If the finding involves mold above the New York threshold, remember that licensed assessors and remediators must be separate.

Working With Your Agent and Attorney

Your real estate agent knows what buyers ask for in your area, and an attorney can explain the disclosure statement. Share your repair records with both early. Ask them how to describe past repairs accurately and what documents to hand over at which stage.

Do not guess about questions on the statement. If you do not know whether a past owner had a flood claim, say so as the form allows and ask your attorney how to answer.

Keep a Home Binder

Collect repair invoices, readings, permits and warranties into one binder or digital folder and hand a copy to your agent. It is easier to answer questions when the paper is already in order.

Local Notes for Brentwood

Brentwood is in the Town of Islip, Suffolk County, and many homes have finished basements where past water events leave marks. Ask the Town of Islip Building Division whether permits were issued for past repairs.

If you are selling in nearby Hauppauge or Islip Terrace, the same disclosure rules apply.
